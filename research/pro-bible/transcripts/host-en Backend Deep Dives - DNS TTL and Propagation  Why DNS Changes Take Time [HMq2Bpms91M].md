# DNS TTL and Propagation — Why DNS Changes Take Time

[0:03] You update a DNS record, refresh your browser, and nothing changes. You check your DNS panel, and the new record is definitely there. But, the old website is still showing up. What's happening? This is DNS propagation, and it confuses nearly every developer the first time they encounter it. The DNS system is designed for high performance through aggressive caching, and that caching is exactly what makes immediate DNS changes impossible. When you update a DNS record, you update it on your authoritative name server, one server, or a small cluster. But, there are thousands of DNS resolvers around the world that have previously cached your old DNS record. They won't query your name server again until that cached record expires. The expiry time is controlled by the TTL, time to live value on the DNS record. A TTL of 3,600 means resolvers can cache that record for 3,600 seconds, 1 hour, before they're required to query again. A TTL of 86,400 means 24 hours. Common misconception, DNS propagation implies DNS records are actively pushed

[1:03] out to servers around the world. That's not how it works. DNS propagation is passive, resolvers keep serving the cached version until their TTL expires, then they re-query the authoritative server and get the new version. The good news, you have control over this. By setting low TTLs before plan changes and high TTLs after things are stable, you can minimize propagation time when you need to move fast. TTL is a number in seconds. It tells DNS resolvers how long they're allowed to cache a record before they must query the authoritative name server again. TTL of 300, resolvers cache this record for 5 minutes. Every 5 minutes, they re-query for the latest value. Changes propagate to most resolvers within 5 to 10 minutes. TTL of 3,600, resolvers cache for 1 hour. Changes take up to 1 hour to propagate. TTL of 86,400, resolvers cache for 24 hours. Changes can take up to 24 hours to propagate worldwide. TTL doesn't guarantee propagation time,

[2:04] it's the maximum. If a resolver cached your record 10 minutes ago when the TTL is 3,600, that resolver won't re-query for another 3,590 seconds. Another resolver that cached it 3,590 seconds ago might re-query in 10 seconds. The propagation time depends on when each resolver last cached the record. The trade-off, low TTL faster changes plus more queries to your name server. High TTL slower changes plus fewer queries, faster performance. For most DNS providers, there's a minimum TTL you can set, usually 1 second for providers like Cloudflare or 60 seconds for others. Default TTL values from major providers, Cloudflare defaults to auto, usually around 300. Route 53 defaults to 300. Many registrars default to 3,600 or 86,400. When you create a new record, no old record to expire TTL doesn't affect how quickly the new record becomes available since there's nothing stale in caches. The first query for a new record goes straight to your authoritative server.
