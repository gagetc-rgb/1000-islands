# Checkout UX Optimization: from 16 form fields to 8 fields
- Source: Baymard Institute (YouTube, en) · 2019-06-21 · 55 min · https://youtu.be/DlQ1wcHFxBo
- Speaker(s) and why they matter: Christian Holst, co-founder and research director of the Baymard Institute, which sells its research database (mentions "Baymard Premium" repeatedly, 5:00, 52:04). Large-scale usability and eye-tracking testing, but a commercial publisher; numbers are Baymard's own.
- Relevance to course: 5 (lesson 5; applies to booking and enquiry forms by analogy) · Freshness matters? statistics date from 2019; Baymard's 2024 page shows updates
- Codes: FORM, UX
- Notes: no sponsor; pitch for the paid database. The topic is e-commerce checkout, so booking forms need adaptation.

## Core thesis
What drives checkout abandonment is the number of form fields users must consider, not the number of steps; most sites can get to about eight fields with many small changes (full name, collapse optional fields, auto-detect city and region from postcode, hide billing address, delay account creation).

## Claims and advice
- [FORM] About 70% of carts are abandoned on average (a meta-average of 41 studies in 2019); about 26% of users had abandoned an order in the past quarter because of a too complicated checkout. (1:07-4:07) — type: evidence (Baymard; 2024 page: average of 50 studies; "17% ... too long / complicated")
- [FORM] The average large site had 5.1 checkout steps in 2019 (unchanged since 2012) and 12.8 form fields (14.9 in 2016); Dell had 45; the best flow had 8. (5:09-13:17) — type: evidence
- [FORM] Number of steps has no consistent correlation with performance; the number of fields does. A/B tests that cut steps also changed fields and copy. (8:13-11:17) — type: evidence
- [FORM] "One-step" accordion checkouts still count as multiple steps to users. (6:10) — type: evidence
- [FORM] There are no "10 commandments"; sites usually need 30-50 small fixes; on average 39 usability issues per checkout among the 60 largest sites. (14:19-16:23) — type: evidence
- [FORM] Name: a single "full name" field; users type their full name in "first name" or fumble between first/middle/last; removing a field is about a 6% cut of fields; splitting later is about 90%+ accurate. (17:24-25:35) — type: evidence
- [FORM] Optional minority fields (address line 2, company, coupon): collapse behind a link with a plus icon; 30% of users stop at address line 2. (25:35-32:43) — type: evidence
- [FORM] City and region: auto-detect from the postcode as soon as the expected length is reached (not on blur); always provide a manual override or abandonment hits users with wrong auto-detect; insert detected fields below the trigger. (32:43-40:53) — type: evidence
- [FORM] Billing address: default to the shipping address and hide the billing fields entirely (not disabled or pre-filled); ask for the shipping address as the one users understand. (40:53-46:59) — type: evidence
- [FORM] Account creation: offer after the order ("delayed account creation") with the password fields on the thank-you page and the benefits stated; guest checkout first. (46:59-52:04) — type: evidence
- [FORM] Result: 16 fields to 8 visible by default. (53:04-54:05) — type: evidence

## Techniques and steps
Profile each field: needed? who needs it? can it be collapsed or detected? Verify with eye-tracking or at least count the fields.

## Disagreements and contrarian points
- Friedman also advocates one thing per page; Baymard says step count is not the driver. Not contradictory: fewer fields per view.

## For the course
Lesson 5: for a travel enquiry or booking form, apply the same logic: ask only what is needed to answer (dates, number of guests, contact), full name field, no repeat entries, optional fields collapsed. Baymard's numbers are checkout-specific; ◆ the 2024 page gives 5.1 steps, 11.3 fields and "most sites need only 8".
