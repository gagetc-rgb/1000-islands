# The Laws of UX - 19 Psychological Design Principles
- Source: Joseph Todaro (YouTube, en) · 2019-06-11 · 10 min · https://youtu.be/fYs2Mdyasuc
- Speaker(s) and why they matter: Joseph Todaro, a designer/educator, narrating the "Laws of UX" collection by product designer Jon Yablonski (used with permission, 0:00). Second-hand: he reads definitions and origin stories (Hick, Fitts, Miller, Gestalt, Doherty, Zeigarnik and others) rather than adding his own experience.
- Relevance to course: 3 (lesson 2: names for common UX laws; a glossary) · Freshness matters? no
- Codes: UX, UI, PERF
- Notes: dense list of definitions with historical dates and attributions that I have not verified one by one. Several "laws" are rules of thumb, not tested design requirements (the video does not say so).

## Core thesis
Many design decisions can be explained by a small set of psychological principles; naming them helps designers justify layouts and flows.

## Claims and advice
- [UX] Aesthetic-usability effect: attractive designs are perceived as easier to use; cited study 1995 by Kurosu and Kashimura with 26 ATM UI variants (0:00). — type: evidence cited (study not checked)
- [PERF][UX] Doherty threshold: productivity rises when system and user interact so neither waits; the video says the 1982 IBM paper set a 400 ms response requirement instead of 2,000 ms. (0:00-1:00) — type: evidence cited (not checked)
- [UX][UI] Fitts's law: time to hit a target depends on distance and size, so make selectable elements large and close. (1:00-2:00) — type: evidence (well-established; matches WCAG 2.5.8 target-size motivation in `PRIMARY_SOURCES.md`)
- [UX] Hick's law: decision time grows with the number and complexity of choices; break complex tasks into steps. (2:00) — type: evidence cited
- [UX] Jakob's law: users spend most time on other sites, so use familiar patterns. (2:00-3:03) — type: opinion/practice
- [UI] Gestalt principles: common region, proximity, similarity, uniform connectedness, Prägnanz (the simplest interpretation wins). (3:03-5:03) — type: evidence cited (psychology)
- [UX] Miller's law: people hold "seven items plus or minus about two" in working memory, so organise content into groups of five to nine. (5:03) — type: practice, **contested as a layout rule** (the original is about memory span, not menu length; not verified here)
- [UX] Occam's razor, Pareto (80% of effects from 20% of causes), Parkinson's law (tasks expand to fill time). (5:03-6:04) — type: opinion (general heuristics, not design findings)
- [UX] Postel's law ("be liberal in what you accept, conservative in what you send"): accept variable input and give clear feedback. (6:04-7:05) — type: practice (applies to forms)
- [UX] Serial position effect: first and last items in a list are remembered best, so put the least important in the middle. (7:05-8:07) — type: evidence cited (memory research)
- [UX] Tesler's law (conservation of complexity): some complexity cannot be removed; make the system, not the user, absorb it. (8:07) — type: opinion
- [UI] Von Restorff (isolation) effect: the item that differs is remembered; make the key action visually distinctive. (8:07-9:08) — type: evidence cited
- [UX] Zeigarnik effect: unfinished tasks are remembered better (basis for progress indicators). (9:08) — type: evidence cited, replication of the original effect is disputed (not verified here)

## Disagreements and contrarian points
- Compared with Nielsen (NN/g keynote): Nielsen insists design rules must be validated by testing with users; this video presents rules without tests. The "magic number seven" use for menus is questioned in the UX literature (not verified here).
