# Julie Grundy – The UX of Form Design: Designing an Effective Form @ UX New Zealand 2017
- Source: Optimal (UX New Zealand conference, YouTube, en) · 2017-11-03 · 28 min · https://youtu.be/hPS7LUW7SlA
- Speaker(s) and why they matter: Julie Grundy, senior UX designer at an e-commerce marketing platform; formerly an agency web designer. Conference talk of guidelines from published research (she cites Luke Wroblewski and Norman Nielsen Group); not original research.
- Relevance to course: 5 (lesson 5) · Freshness matters? mostly timeless
- Codes: FORM, UX, A11Y
- Notes: no sponsor. The Expedia "$12 million" story (2:09-3:11) is a well-known anecdote told without a source here.

## Core thesis
An effective form reduces cognitive load, prevents errors and is human: top-aligned labels, one column, validation per field on blur, plain language, only needed fields, and accessible markup.

## Claims and advice
- [FORM] Expedia story: removing a "company" field on the billing screen (users typed their bank there) raised transactions and revenue, "12 million dollars" in a year. (2:09-3:11) — type: evidence (anecdote, unsourced here)
- [FORM] Forms that follow basic usability guidelines are faster to complete and "almost twice as likely to be submitted without errors". (3:11) — type: evidence (stat not sourced in video)
- [FORM] Top-aligned labels give the fastest completion (less eye movement); keep labels close to inputs; avoid all caps. (6:16-7:19) — type: practice (cites eye-tracking research)
- [FORM] Do not use inline (placeholder) labels: instructions vanish, a pre-filled-looking field gets skipped; blank fields attract attention more (she cites NN/g eye tracking). (7:19-8:19) — type: practice
- [FORM][UI] Put the primary action on the right and the secondary on the left; never use a Reset button; make primary buttons visually dominant. (8:19-9:22, 15:31-16:33) — type: practice
- [FORM] Use one column, including radios and checkboxes. (9:22) — type: practice
- [FORM] Select lists: up to about five options show all; 6-26 in a select; over 26 use search; leave selects blank unless one default fits 90% of users. (9:22, 14:29-15:31) — type: practice
- [FORM] Match field length to expected input; group related fields; prefill; show progress on long forms; use input types (e.g. `tel`) and input masks. (9:22-12:24) — type: practice
- [FORM] Validate per field inline, on blur not on focus; validating on focus "yelling at someone before they've even tried". (13:27-14:29) — type: practice
- [FORM] Help text underneath the field. (15:31) — type: practice
- [FORM][UX] Mark optional fields with "(optional)" rather than marking required with asterisks. (22:42-24:45) — type: opinion
- [FORM][UX] Use clear, human language; button text that says what happens ("find flights"); remove fields that nobody uses (the Duke donation form comment box). (16:33-19:40, 21:42-22:42) — type: practice
- [FORM] Short forms are not always better; long forms work if the questions are contextual and engaging. (22:42) — type: opinion
- [FORM][UI] Material-style floating labels are acceptable if tested and contrast is checked. (26:49-27:50) — type: opinion

## Techniques and steps
Ask for each field: is it needed, is the label clear, is the width right, is the error specific, does the button say what it does.

## For the course
