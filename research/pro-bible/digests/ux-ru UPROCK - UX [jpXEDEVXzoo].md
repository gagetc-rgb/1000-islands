## Core thesis
UX is the logic by which interface elements are arranged for the user's goals (UI is the elements); good UX comes from understanding people (empathy, research, segmentation), forming hypotheses, prototyping, and testing them with the audience, in a repeated cycle.

## Claims and advice
- [UX][UI] "UI are the elements; UX is the logic of how they are arranged"; UX without UI does not exist, and UX is not only for apps but for websites and services too. (3:05-7:09) — type: opinion/definition
- [UX] People focus on a very small area at a time (about 0.5 to 1 degree); attention and memory limits drive layout; speaker cites a neurobiology popularisation lecture. (11:14-17:17) — type: evidence cited (second-hand, **not verified**)
- [UX] Research inputs: data of different kinds (interviews, analytics, competitor analysis), not only interviews; competitor analysis is cheap and fast ("2-3 days", up to 5 with margin); open data can help estimate a market (example of estimating ticket sales from turnover and commission). (23:21-33:33, 1:20:26) — type: practice (anecdotal)
- [UX] Hypothesis cycle: formulate a hypothesis; build a clickable prototype or ship; watch user reaction; feed the result back; repeat. He compares it to science with an Edison "9,999 failed materials" anecdote. (27:26-29:28) — type: practice (the Edison quote is a popular story; not verified)
- [UX] Customer journey map (CJM): list each step the person takes, with their pains, fears and joys, then build question sets per step; segment users (two approaches) rather than designing for everyone. (37:40-49:54) — type: practice
- [UX] Empathy is the core of UX. (49:54) — type: opinion
- [UX][ANALYTICS] A/B testing: run several variants of a page and compare results; usually test a new variant against the old. (1:00:04) — type: practice (see `answers/11-analytics.md` for conditions)
- [UX][UI] Add visual research (references, mood boards) to avoid wooden, repetitive interfaces. (1:00:04) — type: opinion
- [UX] "Your experience is not the users' experience": pause and criticise your work; do "corridor research" by asking relatives and friends neutral questions (do you see this function? where would you find it?). (1:10:15) — type: practice (cheap, biased sample)
- [UX][AIW] "A neural network is compilation, like T9: it offers solutions"; designers should be able to predict what a person feels and focuses on; the tools matter less for someone who truly senses user needs. (1:04:09) — type: opinion
- [UX] Fixing a website built on a no-code tool (Tilda, Webflow) is cheaper and simpler than fixing a mobile app; budget and time determine how much research to do. (1:22:31-1:24:33) — type: practice
- [UX] Data can be misread: a statistics example where changing what counts as a case "improved" a statistic; check interpretation (for example in ad campaigns). (1:06:11-1:08:14) — type: opinion

## Techniques and steps
1. State the hypothesis.
2. Prototype (clickable) or ship a small variant.
3. Observe reaction (including cheap corridor tests).
4. Feed results into the next iteration.

## Disagreements and contrarian points
- Nielsen (NN/g keynote) insists on formal usability testing with real users; this webinar accepts cheap corridor tests with acquaintances, which is the kind of biased sample that research warns about. Both endorse testing; they differ on rigour.
- Claims that the school places students directly in jobs (1:34:49) and that its showcase has a certain number of awards are marketing and not tested.

## For the course
Lesson 2/10: use only the generic loop (hypothesis, prototype, test, iterate), the "your experience is not the users'" caution, and the A/B-testing mention. Not a source for numeric rules. The mobile-app emphasis and sales content limit relevance to a travel website.
