## Core thesis
Interfaces succeed when they follow patterns derived from human psychology; twelve named laws (simplicity, choice, complexity, grouping, memory, targets, time, distinctiveness, 80/20, unfinished tasks, chunking and familiarity) guide layout and flow.

## Claims and advice
- [UX][UI] Law of Prägnanz: people read complex images as the simplest form with least effort; fewer details means faster comprehension. (6:03-8:06) — type: evidence cited (Gestalt), with a form example: a simple form beats a heavily styled one.
- [UX][FORM] Hick's law: more options means longer decision time; the speaker's café example (adding equally rated options makes the brain "collapse"). It does not mean "only three or four elements": it means the fewest functional options, without over-simplifying. (9:06-14:11) — type: practice (example-based)
- [UX] Tesler's law (conservation of complexity): some complexity cannot be reduced; do not remove the minimal fields a form needs. (14:11-16:13) — type: opinion/practice
- [UX][UI] Law of proximity: items near each other read as a group; separate groups by space, and colour and shape can reinforce grouping, but proximity is the strongest cue. (16:13-19:16) — type: practice (consistent with Chainlift and Showalter)
- [UX] Serial position effect: users best remember the first and last items; design the first and last items of lists deliberately. (19:16-22:19) — type: evidence cited
- [UX][UI] Fitts's law: make call-to-action buttons and frequent targets bigger and closer; ergonomics of mouse movement (down and to the right) matter. (22:19-24:21) — type: evidence cited
- [UX] Parkinson's law: tasks expand to fill the time given; shorten tasks to what is needed. (25:23-26:24) — type: opinion
- [UX][UI] Von Restorff (isolation) effect: to emphasise an element, make it differ from the rest. (27:26) — type: evidence cited
- [UX][ANALYTICS] Pareto: 20% of causes give 80% of results; find the elements that generate most results (for example the conversion form) and optimise them first. (28:31-30:31) — type: opinion
- [UX] Zeigarnik effect: unfinished tasks are remembered; Marvel's "to be continued" and progress hooks. (31:32-32:34) — type: opinion/practice
- [UX][FORM] Present information in parts: split a long form into steps ("first the basic information, then next") so the user is not overwhelmed. (33:36-34:37) — type: practice (note: `answers/08-forms-conversion.md` records that Baymard and others find step count matters and single-page checkout can work; the evidence is mixed)
- [UX] Jakob's law: users prefer interfaces that work like those they already know; do not change patterns (for example the buy button position) without a strong reason. (35:38-36:38) — type: practice
