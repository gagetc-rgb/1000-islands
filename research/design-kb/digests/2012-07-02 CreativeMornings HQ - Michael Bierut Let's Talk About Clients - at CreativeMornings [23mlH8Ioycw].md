# Michael Bierut: Let's Talk About Clients
- Source: CreativeMornings HQ (NYC talk) · 2012-07-02 · 49 min · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=23mlH8Ioycw
- Speaker and why they matter: Michael Bierut, partner at Pentagram New York, 30 years of practice at the time. One of the most respected graphic designers on client relationships.
- Relevance to us: 5 for module 1 (process and client) · Freshness: timeless (2012, about human dynamics, not tools)
- Note: the last ~15 minutes are a tribute to ten named clients and Q&A; the principles are in 0:03-27:36 and the Q&A answers at 36:45-48:58.

## Core thesis
Clients are the best part of the process, and the biggest lever is choosing and keeping good clients, giving them your time, and being honest with them. Good work for good clients leads to more good work; bad work for bad clients never leads anywhere good.

## Claims and advice
- [CL] Designers who dream of working without clients are mistaken; clients provoke the work. He would "go insane" without them. (2:06) — opinion
- [CL] Good designers get genuinely interested in what the client cares about (nuclear disarmament, football); his work improved once he did. (3:07) — practice
- [CL] Clients of famous studios are no better or worse than anyone's; people show up as whatever they already were. (5:09) — opinion/evidence from experience
- [CL] Best clients either love design or genuinely don't care and just trust; the worst are in between: interested enough in design to worry, afraid of it, or determined not to repeat a famous failure. (9:15-10:16) — opinion
- [CL] The decision-maker can be anyone in the org (junior marketer to owner) as long as they are confident in their judgment and in yours. (9:15) — opinion
- [CL] Never "educate the client". He has never met a client who needed educating; a flawed relationship means he was not educated enough about their business. Client's job is the business, his job is design. (10:16) — opinion, strongly held
- [CL][VIS-T] In meetings avoid talking typefaces, white space, composition, color; the conversation is 99% about their business and goals, 1% about design tools. He fusses over them privately for hours. (11:17) — practice
- [CL] Four traits of a good client: brains (smarter than you), passion (for their own work, not necessarily design), trust (you can't prove most design suggestions with a calculator; low-trust clients over-rely on focus groups), courage (not heroics: e.g. calling "Bob" in operations right away instead of logging his objection). (12:19-16:25) — opinion
- [CL] Good clients are two-way: chemistry with a particular designer develops in tandem; a great client for you may not be for a partner. (16:25-17:26) — evidence
- [CL][BIZ] "Good work for good clients leads to more good work; bad work for bad clients leads to more bad work." Taking a bad job to get a foot in a new field leads to more bad jobs in that field. (17:26-19:26) — opinion, claims 30 years of confirmation
- [CL][BIZ] Bad clients consume more time (change decisions, re-open, new information); good clients are easy to take for granted. Reverse the allocation: lavish time on good clients, minimize it on bad. (19:26-20:28) — practice
- [CL] Practice: profile clients early, keep a "watch list", and progressively replace bad clients; you cannot be too picky, but you can shift the mix. (20:28-22:30) — practice
- [CL] What designers owe clients: loyalty, honesty, dedication, tenacity. (22:30) — opinion
- [CL] Honesty examples: tell a prospective client their project may not help them and propose a cheaper effective alternative; present one option "I think it's great, if not I'll come back"; bring unfinished work and solve it in the room; say "someone else might do this better than me". Builds trust. (23:31-25:33) — practice
- [CL] Once you find a great client, never let them go (not exclusivity: keep trust). (25:33) — practice
- [CL][P] Q: how to explain design to clients: acquaint yourself with their content (read all of the text they give you); in presentations point to where the design reflects their own content. No one else has read it, so you get ahead. (36:45-38:48) — practice
- [CL] Keep the discussion about what normal people will see (the evidence in front of them), not type bases or strategy. (39:48) — opinion
- [CL] Q: can you turn a bad client good? Probably not, but you can tap their good side; e.g. a rule-following client who "walks through walls" for an agreed rule. (41:49-42:50) — opinion
- [CL][BIZ] Biggest favor to yourself: be in a position to walk away from jobs. Walking away politely ("I don't think we're connecting; here are other designers") sometimes wakes the client up, sometimes enrages them; he has paid to be released. (44:56-48:58) — practice
- [CL] Limits help: he responds better to interesting constraints than "do something pretty" briefs; sometimes he invents a problem to solve. (47:58) — practice

## Techniques and steps
1. Learn the client's content before presenting; tie design decisions to their content.
2. Screen for brains, passion, trust, courage.
3. Time-box bad clients, over-invest in good ones.
4. Be honest: one strong option, cheaper alternatives, referrals.
5. Keep ability to walk away; exit politely with referrals.

## Tools and resources
Books mentioned: his own "79 Short Essays on Design"; Dale Carnegie's "How to Win Friends and Influence People" (people like talking about themselves); Paula Scher's "Make It Bigger" (client passage).

## Quotes
- "I never ever talk about educating the client. I hate that phrase." (10:16)
- On trust (paraphrase, 14:21): design suggestions cannot be proven like other business advice, so trust is what lets the work happen.

## Disagreements and contrarian points
Against "educate the client", against designers' war stories, against Sagmeister's year without clients. Against the myth of the designer as artist without commissioners.

## For 1000 Islands
The owner is the client: find what he actually cares about (his guests, his islands), read his own materials before presenting, do not argue about fonts and colors in meetings, bring one strong direction and a cheaper alternative, and say honestly when something is not worth the money (the 35m IDR scope). Also relevant to our "scope = offer minimum" rule. Be ready to say what is not our job.
