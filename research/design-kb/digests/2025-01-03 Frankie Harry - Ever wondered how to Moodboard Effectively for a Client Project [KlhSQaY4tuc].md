## Core thesis
Before moodboarding, onboard the client with a paid deposit, a contract and a ~35-question questionnaire, then analyse competitors visually and build three moodboards (modern, traditional, bold) including one the client probably will not choose. The client reacts to visuals, picks one and borrows from the others, and the final board is a blend that gets signed off.

## Claims and advice
- [P][CL] His onboarding sequence before design: discovery call, proposal, 50% deposit invoice before any work, contract, then a shared Notion questionnaire (about 35 questions: target audience, new identity, competitors). (0:00-1:00) — practice
- [CL] Clients who fill the questionnaire thoroughly show care and invest more in the outcome. (1:00) — opinion
- [MB][CL] Convert the brief into keywords for how the identity and audience should feel (example list: approachable, relatable, premium, empowering, intentional, exclusive, welcoming, simple, reliable, authoritative). (2:03) — practice
- [MB] Words alone are weak: one word like "authoritative" can map to two completely different visuals; do not feel bound by keywords when sourcing images. (2:03-3:03) — opinion
- [MB][CL] Make a couple of moodboards, including one the client might not want. In his experience a client chose the one they "didn't think they wanted" because it attracted the target audience better and differed from competitors. (3:03) — evidence (anecdote)
- [REF][P] Competitor visual analysis comes before moodboarding: for each competitor, screenshot logo, headline typography, illustrations, textures, colour palette, iconography, layout and buttons into three template slides, then write a short synopsis (typography, illustrations, textures, colours, logo style). (3:03-10:14) — practice
- [REF][MB] Place each competitor on a spectrum with sliders: modern/classic, simple/ornate, playful/serious, informal/formal, affordable/premium, inclusive/exclusive, feminine/masculine, quiet/loud. The same spectrum is filled in by the client on a call, which gives measurable targets for the boards. (8:12, 12:18) — practice
- [MB][CL] The client's spectrum for this project: modern, playful, simple, between exclusive and inclusive, 6 on affordable, slightly informal, slightly masculine, slightly loud, 8 for rugged, a bit youthful. Each board states how close it sits to that spectrum. (12:18) — evidence/example
- [MB] Three directions for this client: traditional, modern (closest to what the client asked for), and bold/retro; named "intriguing digital", "artistic juxtaposition" and "youthful rebellion". (2:03, 17:29-21:32) — practice
- [REF][TOOLS] Cosmos (cosmos.so): curation site for designers, AI-assisted search by feeling ("noisy gradient mellow calm feeling", "mono logo type modern"); good for searching a feeling not just logos. Save images to downloads, drag into Illustrator. (12:18-13:20) — practice
- [MB][VIS-L] Board building: clipping masks in Illustrator, arrange images "in a nice order to evoke a feeling"; the board is not literal, "just having a bit of fun"; if the client picks a board the logo does not have to use the exact type shown, only the feeling. (16:27-17:29) — practice
- [MB] Finding references that match a vision in your head is slow ("mood boards can take a long time"). (14:20) — opinion
- [MB][CL] Each board gets a name, a short rationale tied to the brief and the competitors (e.g. "leans into competitors' styles but a black palette with high-energy colours differentiates"), typography notes, illustration notes, textures, colours, a potential palette and the spectrum. (18:29-21:32) — practice
- [MB][CL] The "wrong" board is useful: gets an unwanted direction out of the way before designing, so you do not unknowingly present something like it later. (20:32) — practice
- [P] Document structure: contents, competitor visuals, mood boards, and an empty "final mood board" page for amendments after feedback. (5:07, 22:32) — practice
- [CL] On the call: show competitors and ask what the client likes about them and whether to lean into or away from them, then ask what they like and dislike about each board. Client preferred the modern board but liked elements of the other two and wanted grungier textures. (22:32-25:35) — evidence
- [P] Next step: build a "final mood board" mainly from B plus elements from A and C, send for review and sign-off; then creation starts (next video). (25:35) — practice
- [MB] Warning: do not be too literal and box yourself in with styles; a mood board is a feeling. (25:35) — opinion

## Techniques and steps
1. Onboard: call, proposal, deposit, contract, questionnaire.
2. Read the questionnaire; extract feeling keywords; get the spectrum values from the client.
3. Competitor analysis: 3 competitors, screenshot visuals, write synopses, place on the spectrum.
4. Decide three directions: traditional, modern (likely client pick), bold; one "wild" one.
5. Source references (Cosmos), build each board, name it, annotate with brief-linked rationale and spectrum.
6. Email client; present on call; ask what they like and dislike per board and about competitors.
7. Build the final board as a blend; client signs off; begin design.

## Disagreements and contrarian points
Makes three boards (contrast with Flux Academy: two, rarely three). Includes a deliberately off-brief board as a feature. Uses competitor analysis before moodboards, which sets direction relative to the market.

## For 1000 Islands
Steal the competitor analysis: screenshot 3-4 competing Thousand Islands / lake-region tour and lodging sites (type, imagery, colour, layout, buttons) and place them on a spectrum (classic/modern, premium/affordable, quiet/loud). Ask the owner to fill in the same spectrum; this turns taste into numbers and avoids font/colour debates. Three boards is a lot for our offer-minimum scope: two plus one off-brief "option" is a reasonable compromise. Collect feeling-based references via Cosmos or Are.na. Use a "final board" page for the owner sign-off.
