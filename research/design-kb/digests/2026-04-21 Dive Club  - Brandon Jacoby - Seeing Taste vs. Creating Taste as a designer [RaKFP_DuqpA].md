## Core thesis
Everyone can see taste; what separates tastemakers is knowing when to follow existing patterns and when to break them, and when to go deep with prompts versus when "not everything needs to be new". Taste is a muscle fostered by tinkering and decisiveness; AI removes tooling limits but not the need for judgment, and "good enough" is cheap, so the value is in pushing past it.

## Claims and advice
- [AIS] Taste is not an aesthetic checklist (minimal, left-aligned bold type); designers have become homogeneous, "designing for other designers". Taste is more about the journey than the result. (22:20) — opinion
- [AIS] Seeing taste is common ("you know it when you see it"); creating taste means knowing when to follow patterns, when to blaze trails, when to be quiet. This balance, "zooming in and out", is what AI cannot supply. (25:22-26:25) — opinion
- [AIS] Taste is partly innate and partly a muscle: like athletic talent, still needs training. Environment shapes it; copying only the visuals fails because taste goes deeper than surface. (23:21) — opinion
- [Q][P] Utilitarian flows (onboarding, growth) have patterns that show up in 90% of products for a reason; know when to reinvent and when to follow. He calls this the number one trait for junior designers. Warns the industry also over-reverts to the mean. (8:08-9:11) — opinion from experience
- [Q] Decisiveness and judgment are the enduring design skill; "great design has always been great design agnostic of the tools". He calls decisiveness a keyword for 2026. (3:02-4:04, 13:13) — opinion
- [Q] Visual design requires feeling and empathy ("how is someone going to feel when they look at this"); basic broken principles (weird alignment, text walls) prevent calm, and an earth-tone palette alone will not fix it. Fundamentals still change how people feel; vibe-coded products skip them. (20:18-21:20) — opinion
- [AI] The biggest AI win in client work: building your own design tools for a specific purpose; cites John Lasseter's line "the technology inspired the art, the art challenged the technology". (15:16-16:16) — practice/opinion
- [AI] His stack: Figma for UI exploration ("dragging rectangles around is still the best form of expression"), Claude Code for building, Figma Make for creative blocks, an agent for logistics and email. Example: Claude produced a particle effect assembling into feature-card icons he could not have coded. (13:13-17:16) — practice
- [AI][VIS-M] Advice: when stuck, ask Claude how to do the thing; people often do not know what to ask for (e.g. a WebGL 3D render). (17:16-18:16) — practice
- [AI][AIS] Who is amplified most by AI: people who already "run through walls", refuse to accept blockers, and question why something is a requirement; people who externalize blockers will not be rescued by AI. (18:16-20:18) — opinion
- [P] Start fast with something bad: the first version of everything is "absolute garbage", but starting begins the snowball; many "wrong" ideas help a non-designer founder discover what they actually think. More artifacts accelerate the founder's thinking. (43:41-45:43) — practice
- [AIS][AI] Tinkering on a canvas between prompts yields "some weird C thing" you did not plan; vibe-coding by prompt alone skips these discoveries. Crossing the "probably good enough" line is where it appears. (45:43-47:46) — opinion
- [BIZ][EMO] Software is "a form of hospitality"; when building is cheap, people still choose what to use based on how it feels, like choosing where to live. (29:30) — opinion
- [Q][BIZ] "Craft meets consequence": craft and commercial impact are not mutually exclusive; do not design only for other designers' portfolios, nor ship only to metrics. (35:35-37:37) — opinion
- [AIS][VIS-L] Good enough is the copy-paste "modern farmhouse"; hotels and restaurants show businesses built on interior quality. Taste can be accessible: Apple, Uber. Great can also come from "ruthless simplification". (47:46-49:47) — opinion
- [BIZ] Zero-to-one: pick collaborators you enjoy; trust your own ideas; copying great products is "an okay place to start" for blank-canvas paralysis. (41:40-43:41) — practice
- [BIZ][CL] Positioning as a solo practice: sells taste to founders working on net-new things; mentors outside design (Owen Jennings, Block) taught him most about business. (28:29-34:34) — opinion
- [BIZ] Working at X: no committees, instant decisions, question every requirement; he found micro-decision design without constraints "a big hill to climb". (1:00-4:04) — evidence (anecdote)

## Techniques and steps
1. Start from an existing good pattern or reference, but expect to change it (43:41).
2. Make the first terrible version fast; show many artifacts to the client (44:42-45:43).
3. Tinker between prompts on a canvas to find an unplanned "C" option (45:43-46:44).
4. For utilitarian flows, check known patterns first; only reinvent where it matters (9:11).
5. Build small custom tools with AI for your own purpose (15:16).

## Disagreements and contrarian points
Rejects the "delete Figma / Claude Code only" versus "AI can't do taste" debate: "it doesn't really matter", tools change but judgment stays. Argues taste is not minimal-and-clean by default. Self-interested: sells taste services.

## For 1000 Islands
Most useful as a mindset: for utilitarian parts (booking, enquiry, navigation) use proven patterns; spend originality on the photographic, emotional layer. Show the owner many rough artifacts early so he can react ("help them think"). "Software as hospitality" fits a travel site. Avoid shipping merely "good enough" (modern-farmhouse sameness); but upper-middle (not luxury) audience matches his point that great can be accessible. Does not give visual rules.
