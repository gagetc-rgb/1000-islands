# Michael Bierut: How to Think Like a Designer
- Source: Design Indaba (conference talk, Cape Town) · 2016-05-18 · 39 min · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RanfCx18gi4
- Speaker(s) and why they matter: Michael Bierut, partner at Pentagram New York, about 40 years in practice. Respected practitioner; this is a portfolio talk of case stories, not a method talk.
- Relevance to us: 3 · Freshness matters? Timeless
- Notes: Despite the title, there is no systematic "how to think" framework; the lessons are implicit in anecdotes. Captions garble some names (Renzo Piano, Massimo Vignelli as "masimo velli", Christoph Niemann as "Kristoff nean", Stefan Sagmeister); verify any name before reuse. The ballot story and the MAD logo story are told for effect and simplified.

## Core thesis
Design matters in small and large ways: a ballot can swing an election, a delivery box can make people happy, a library mural gives a librarian a daily ritual. His working lessons, shown through projects: fit the design to the real use, combine old and new, listen when the client rejects an idea, and use a constraint or a system to generate variety.

## Claims and advice
- [EMO] His career started at age 7 from noticing that the "L" in a Clark forklift logo lifts the "A": a small moment of pleasure and surprise put in by a designer. (1:04-2:05) — anecdote
- [Q] Design is not harmless: a confusing butterfly ballot in Palm Beach County, Florida (2000) put presidential candidates on two sides and punched holes did not line up; about 1,000 people claimed they voted for Buchanan by mistake and Gore lost Florida by about 700 votes by some counts (disputed). (3:05-4:06) — evidence (his telling)
- [EMO][BIZ] Nuts.com packaging (client Jeff Braverman): anti-identity, hand-painted "nuts" lettering, a hand-drawn alphabet ("nutcase"), cartoon family characters by Christoph Niemann; brown cardboard boxes that look unlike Amazon or the post office; sales went up, no added manufacturing cost, more recyclable. (5:09-7:11) — evidence (self-reported)
- [EMO][VIS-L] Saks Fifth Avenue: to be timeless and fashionable, they went back through the company's own history to a 1973 script logo, cleaned it up, placed it in a square, and divided it into 64 squares; the zoomed-in squares act as modern-art patterns (60 different packages, no two alike, computer-generated patterns on bags, awnings, windows). "Newness" needs something old to manipulate. (7:11-10:13) — practice
- [VIS-T] Cathedral of St John the Divine: revive a typeface dated almost to the cathedral's founding year (designer name in captions as "Frederick gouty", probably Goudy; verify), cleaned up, and pair it with a contemporary sans so "old and new coexist"; copy has humour ("refresh thy homepage", "I shall not poop"). (11:15-13:18) — practice
- [VIS-L] New York Times building: instead of one big sign blocking views, 900 tiny teardrop signs on ceramic rods, readable from outside, nearly see-through from inside; inside, every room sign differs using photos from the archive to give the glass building some "old Times" character. (13:18-18:24) — practice
- [P][CL] Museum of Arts and Design: he fell for his first idea (a continuous-line "A+D" logo) and kept pushing it through about seven meetings; the client kept saying they did not like it. Eventually he listened, restarted late in the process, and found a simpler idea from the site (square building on a circle: squares and circles making "MAD"), which then generated a full typeface and products. (18:24-22:28) — practice/lesson
- [P] City wayfinding (NYC DOT "WalkNYC"): user interviews and map-drawing tests, build on what exists (Vignelli's 1970 subway system, Helvetica) with small tweaks ("Helvetica DOT" with round dots, adjusted icons), with local wayfinding and product-design partners; people are observed getting less lost. (22:28-26:34) — evidence
- [VIS-L][DS] MIT Media Lab: one modular 7x7 (49 squares) grid generates the lab's logo and almost unlimited logos for the two dozen research groups; a system, not a single mark. (27:36-29:41) — practice
- [P][CL][EMO] Robin Hood Foundation school libraries: library is "5% of the space but 100% of the students"; scope grew from a logo to commissioning murals by top illustrators (Christoph Niemann, Stefan Sagmeister, others), done for free work; a librarian turns on the lights on the kids' portraits last "to remind myself why I come to work every day". Lesson: design can give people a daily ritual. (30:47-38:56) — evidence/anecdote

## Techniques and steps
1. Look at the client's history and site for the starting material (Saks old logo; MAD from the building's square-in-circle).
2. When the client keeps rejecting an idea across several meetings, restart, even late.
3. Build a system (grid, modular units) that lets the client produce variants without you.
4. Find the real-world use (ballot layout, wayfinding tests, signage seen through glass) and test the design against it.

## Disagreements and contrarian points
Self-critique: his own first idea was wrong and the client was right; admits designers think their first idea is best. Against the notion of design as cosmetic.

## For 1000 Islands
Little process guidance, but three usable ideas: (1) build from what already exists on the islands (history, local materials, existing signage and place names) rather than invent; (2) a small modular system can give many consistent pages; (3) if the owner keeps resisting a direction, restart rather than push. Also: small delight moments are memorable.
