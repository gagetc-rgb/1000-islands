# Usability Testing: How to Do-It-Yourself with Steve Krug

- Speaker(s) and why they matter: Steve Krug, usability consultant (~25 years), author of "Don't Make Me Think" and "Rocket Surgery Made Easy". The standard source for cheap, do-it-yourself usability testing. Commercial interest: sells his books (free test script and forms on his website, sensible.com).
- Relevance to us: 5 for module 7 (testing) · Freshness matters? Timeless (method); tool names (Camtasia, usertesting.com, GoToMeeting, WebEx) are dated
- Notes: Transcript is a live talk; the first ~8 min are personal chat and audience polling (skip), minutes 8-23 are usability-problem anecdotes. The core method is from 23:29 onward. Includes a live demo test with a volunteer on a New Zealand immigration site (27:34-38:46). Captions are mostly clean; "Camtasia" is a screen recorder.

## Core thesis
Usability testing is one of the cheapest and most effective ways to improve what you build, and teams should do it themselves, routinely, with three users per round and without labs, reports or statistics. It also ends endless opinion fights, because everyone sees the same real users struggle.

## Claims and advice
- [TEST] Usability = an average or below-average person can figure out how to use a thing for its purpose "without it being more trouble than it's worth". (8:10-9:11) — opinion/definition
- [TEST][P] Design arguments are "religious debates": developers, designers and marketing hold deeply held personal beliefs based on who they are (developers like complexity, designers like hairlines and grey-on-grey type); market research does not settle contextual UX questions; "it depends". (18:25-22:29) — opinion
- [TEST] We are not our users, we underestimate how diverse they are ("all use is idiosyncratic") and we cannot remember what they do not know; testing lets you watch someone else have that experience. (22:29-23:29) — opinion
- [TEST] A usability test is watching people try to use what you made while they think aloud, not a focus group asking opinions; watch for "question marks forming over their head". (23:29-24:31) — practice
- [TEST] Common excuses (cost, time, finding users, fear, ignorance) are answered by doing it yourself in a simple form. (7:10-8:10, 24:31) — opinion
- [TEST] Test three users per round, not five or eight: you will find as many problems as you can fix, recruiting is easy, and you need no statistics. No lab, no two-way mirror: use screen sharing to another room, record with a screen recorder (Camtasia), skip entrance/exit questionnaires. (39:46-41:47) — practice
- [TEST] Replace the 80-page report with a team debrief right after the test and a two-page-max email with bullets: what was tested, what was shown (link to prototype), tasks, the worst problems, what will be done. (41:47) — practice
- [TEST][P] Maxim 1, start earlier than you think: test before it is finished, even a competitor's site, the old version, a napkin sketch, wireframes, a "garden path" prototype. Testing someone else's site is ethical and gives a fully worked design: "they left the keys in the car". (42:50-45:51) — practice
- [TEST] Maxim 2, a morning a month: pick one morning a month (e.g. the "third Thursday") with whatever is available; it is doable and decouples testing from slipping deliverables. (45:51-46:54) — practice
- [TEST] Maxim 3, recruit loosely and grade on a curve: early on, people outside your target audience find the gross problems too; keep the bar low ("knows where the back button is"). Still, English-speaking facilitator and observers for language-heavy tests. (25:32-26:32, 46:54-47:55) — practice
- [TEST][CL] Maxim 4, make it a spectator sport: invite stakeholders, developers and designers to observe and debrief together; their shared experience beats reports. Provide good snacks. (47:55-49:57) — practice
- [TEST] Maxim 5, focus relentlessly on a small number of the most serious problems: each observer writes the top three problems per session and brings three of nine to the debrief; avoid the low-hanging-fruit trap. (49:57-51:00) — practice
- [TEST] Maxim 6, tweak, do not redesign: ask "what is the smallest change that may solve the observed problem", make it now; he lists nine reasons, the last being that redesign means many people and many meetings. (51:00-52:02) — practice
- [TEST] Remote testing works with the same method over screen sharing with good audio (voice over IP, not speakerphone) after about 8-9 in-person tests; unmoderated services like usertesting.com return video in about an hour. (53:02-54:04) — practice
- [TEST] Verbatim script: read your intro word for word; say you are testing the site, not the person, ask them to think aloud, ask for honest reactions, get recording permission, ask a few background questions, then a first-impressions narrative without clicking, then a task (demo: do you need a visa to give a talk in New Zealand as a US citizen, without using search). (27:34-36:43) — practice
- [TEST][Q] Demo findings: even a well-regarded site (New Zealand immigration) had too many options on the compare page, snippets not enough to choose, and a navigation loop; Krug attributes much of it to a "wicked problem" (legal complexity). (37:43-39:46) — evidence

## Techniques and steps
1. Recruit 3 people loosely; schedule one morning; set up screen sharing in another room for observers.
2. Read the intro script verbatim; ask for permission to record; ask a few background questions.
3. Show the page and ask what they make of it without clicking; then give a task, tell them to think aloud, do not help.
4. Each observer writes the top three problems per participant.
5. Debrief immediately, choose the three most serious problems, pick the smallest fix for each, email a two-page bullet summary.

## Disagreements and contrarian points
Against the "test five users" convention (Nielsen's number) and against big usability reports, statistics, labs and recruiting only the exact target audience. Against redesign in favour of tweaks.

## For 1000 Islands
Very usable for Stage 1/2 checks: ask 3 friends outside the project to try the clickable wireframes with one task each ("find and request a private boat day for 4 people"), think aloud, record the screen, and debrief with the top 3 problems. Also test a competitor travel site the same way before designing. A one-morning test costs almost nothing.
