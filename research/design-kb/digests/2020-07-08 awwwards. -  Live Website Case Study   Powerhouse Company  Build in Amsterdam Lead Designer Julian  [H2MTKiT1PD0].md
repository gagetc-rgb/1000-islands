## Core thesis
A distinctive site comes from a strategy phase first: a one-line brand claim ("We give meaning to space") became the logo logic, the menu, the grid and the animation speed, so every design and dev choice could be traced to two strategy documents. Build in Amsterdam treats UX and UI as one discipline and makes one considered design instead of presenting several.

## Claims and advice
- [P] Before design they ran an extensive strategy phase producing a strategic brand positioning and a brand claim ("We give meaning to space"); "every design choice and every development choice" traces to these two key documents. (4:12-6:17) — practice (case)
- [P][CL] The brand claim is a consumer-facing line with a why/how/what and a manifesto; it should have character and be easy to grasp. (5:15) — practice
- [VIS-T] Logo combines a serif and a sans-serif, with small serif elements trimmed (thinner at top and bottom of letters) to give a name-only logo some uniqueness; identity applied to letterhead, cards, socials; heavy use of white space as a hint of the strategy ("giving meaning to space by removing a lot"). (6:17-8:19) — practice
- [P] UX phase called the "incubator": wireframes defined space as a big open field like a museum room, a "bendable grid" of projects; a colleague (Aurelia, garbled) proposed using the brand claim itself as the menu (the sentence: "We give meaning to space" with words acting as links and project filters such as towers, offices, living). (8:19-10:22) — practice (case)
- [P][VIS-L] "UX is UI": every choice in the UX phase is a design choice; final design looks close to the wireframes. (12:25-13:40) — opinion
- [VIS-M] Animations are slow and airy to give a spacious feel; kept simple so they stay timeless; "it's about quality and timelessness". The flat-looking grid is actually WebGL. (13:40-16:47) — practice
- [VIS-L] An algorithm lays out the project grid: designers mark which project is most important and the layout calculates positions around it; it also adapts to screen ratio (horizontal oval on desktop, more landscape circle on mobile). (14:41-15:41) — practice
- [VIS-L] Filtering zooms out to show all cases; filtered results switch to a plain, orderly grid on the philosophy that someone who filters is looking for something specific and wants less noise; light parallax keeps varied photo ratios interesting. (15:41-22:57) — practice
- [VIS-I][VIS-L] Reuse only a few image ratios (square, portrait) across the entire site: it feels unconsciously organized, magazine-like, and the client has to crop only one or two versions of each image. (22:57-23:57) — practice
- [EMO][CL] Content idea from the strategy: give every person at the firm a voice, with pop-up profiles of team members linked to their projects, producing a "spiderweb" navigation, and a "join the conversation" comment area under projects where architects answer questions; the result felt like a social platform though it does not look like one. (10:22-12:25, 25:02) — practice (case)
- [P] Custom router allowing stacked, multiple overlay levels (rare); a close button that fills to show scroll progress on very long project pages. (19:49-21:56) — practice
- [DS] Client toolkit: pages built from components (slideshow, floor plan, etc.) in a "Lego" system on one simple grid, so any combination always looks good; they also did art direction of all team portraits. (18:48-19:49, 17:48) — practice
- [P] Team: about 8 people from the studio (creative director, project lead, designer, wireframes, development, etc.) plus client team; whole project about 9 months to a year including content entry. (26:07-27:19) — evidence
- [Q] Primary users: architects (job seekers and inspiration), future clients, real estate developers, government institutions; so the site must be a presentable portfolio of everything. (28:20-30:23) — practice
- [TEST] User testing happens in several ways: wireframes as a first concept check (hard, since testers see through grey blocks), then Figma prototypes sent by link to test with professionals and friends; for ongoing partnerships, design is adjusted from usage data. (31:25-32:25) — practice
- [AI][TOOLS] Opinion on Webflow: tried and got lost; not worth the time on large multi-page projects; you still need a developer for non-standard things and designers and developers should be divided. (33:27-34:28) — opinion (2020, tool-dependent)
- [VIS-M][TOOLS] Prototypes animation in Figma (about 70% of the final) and sometimes Keynote; After Effects by motion designers; simple functional animation does not need many effects. (34:28-35:31) — practice (tool-dependent)
- [P] What fails after wireframing is usually content (poor product shots, mismatched images), not the wireframe logic; fix by controlling content. (35:31-36:32) — opinion
- [VIS-C] The logo's blue (inspired by Yves Klein's intense blue) was toned down on the site; originally used everywhere but it "screamed", pulling attention from the projects, which are the core. "Too much identity" can hurt the function; keep it minimal. (37:33-39:35) — practice
- [Q] The unusual minimal menu: an earlier more obvious version existed; they accepted that users have to get used to it and kept a backup plan. Taking risks on unusual choices. (36:32-38:34) — practice
- [MOB][P] Design mobile and desktop at the same time; here many users are architects on large screens. (40:38) — practice
- [REF][P] Where does newness come from? From the boundaries defined in strategy; the client's specific constraints force something unique. Taking something you like from a trend is fine, "stealing" is not copying a trend: results become unique because of the boundaries. (41:40-42:42) — opinion
- [P] "Do not think inside the borders of what's possible"; design freely and challenge developers; no limitation hit on this project. (43:46-44:47) — opinion
- [Q][BIZ] No secret recipe for awards: amount of work shows; extra hours, "delete our darlings", good team. They do not design to win awards: design for the client and audience and the award follows. (45:51-48:55) — opinion

## Techniques and steps
1. Strategy: positioning plus a one-line brand claim, before any wireframe.
2. Turn the claim into structure (menu, grid, motion speed).
3. UX phase decides space, navigation, content types; treat it as design.
4. Make one design; avoid presenting three.
5. Limit image ratios; build page components on one grid.
6. Prototype in Figma, test links with friends and pros; after launch adjust from usage.
7. Tone down the identity color if it competes with the content.

## Disagreements and contrarian points
Against presenting several concepts. Against Webflow for large professional projects. Against forced use of the brand identity colour. Against designing within what is "technically possible".

## For 1000 Islands
Strong match for process: write a one-line brand claim for the owner first and derive navigation and motion from it, keep to two image ratios across the site (cheap and tidy for a photo-led site), tone down identity color so photography leads, and present one considered direction rather than three (aligns with the owner-facing sign-off pipeline, but check against the planned Stage 2 "shotgun" of 4-6 hero variants, which this source argues against for the final choice). Slow, airy motion for a calm-travel feel.
