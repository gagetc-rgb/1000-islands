## Core thesis
Models are "smart yet stupid" at once: strong on benchmarks, but real-world agent output often cannot be used as is. Because production of ideas and code is becoming nearly free, the cap on productivity is human (and agent) evaluation and synthesis, so the future skill is taste, noise management and orchestrating agents that scrutinize each other.

## Claims and advice
- [AI] Working definition of an agent (credited to Simon Willison): "an LLM running tools in a loop". 2025 is the year development became agentic. (1:02) — practice, definition
- [AI] Demo of the "smart yet stupid" gap: asked to play rock-paper-scissors with the model going first, the user wins three times; the model then rationalizes (calculates 3.7% odds, blames "first move advantage", suggests more rounds) instead of seeing the trap. (3:08-5:13) — evidence, anecdote
- [AI] AI shipped to production fails visibly: a New Zealand meal-planner app suggested a chlorine recipe; Google AI Overviews told users to add glue to pizza sauce and eat a rock a day. (7:13-8:15) — evidence
- [Q][AI] METR study (his cited data): of 102 agent runs on real open-source repos, 63 failed tests, 4 needed under 15 minutes to fix, 35 needed more than 15 minutes; none were mergeable as is. Agents write functionally correct code that fails on test coverage, formatting, linting, code quality. (9:16-10:18) — evidence, tool-dependent
- [AI] Benchmarks may overestimate real-world performance because models max out tests (METR's own caveat). (11:19-12:20) — evidence
- [AI] A better measure: task complexity measured by human completion time; METR reports it has doubled about every 7 months over six years; if extrapolated, agents could handle days-to-weeks software tasks "in under a decade". He is skeptical and notes plateaus: agents stop improving on a task however long they run. (13:21-16:24) — evidence, forecast
- [AI] More context is not better: "context rot" (Chroma's term): the more tokens in the window, the worse recall; vibe-coding tools work for generations 1-2 then introduce regressions as fast as value. Treat context as a finite resource with diminishing returns. (17:24-18:25) — evidence/practice
- [AI] Manage context: compress history into significant events, infer the user's intent, pass only what is needed; he points to Anthropic's blog post on context engineering for agents. (18:25-19:26) — practice
- [AI][P] Spec-driven development (write a PRD first, then generate) is waterfall again: you cannot write the perfect spec for novel work before exploring the problem space; blaming the user for a "bad spec" ignores that. (19:26-21:29) — opinion
- [AI] Agents are like "100 interns" who score better on standardized tests than you but do not know where they are going; the job becomes managing them. Automation tends to grow an industry rather than shrink it, but individual contributors shift from producing to managing machines. (21:29-23:32) — opinion
- [AIS][AI] On divergent-thinking tasks AI is strong: a Cornell and Wharton study found GPT-4 ideas beat 90.6% of student ideas and AI ideas were seven times more likely to rank in the top 10%. (23:32-25:33) — evidence
- [AIS] Conclusion: cost of producing ideas goes toward zero, so a person's edge becomes building taste and evaluating; "your productivity will be capped by your ability to evaluate and synthesize". (25:33-26:35) — opinion
- [AI] Proposed fix: orchestrate teams of agents with shared goals but conflicting interests (PM, UX designer, senior engineer sub-agents running in parallel); he considers one blog example "pitching it a bit too hard". (26:35-27:37) — practice, partly skeptical
- [AI] Backing theory: from the book "The Enigma of Reason" (Mercier and Sperber), reasoning evolved socially for argument and scrutiny, not solitary reflection, so agents debating from different perspectives could turn noise into signal; criticizes single-agent chain-of-thought. Compares to "institutionalized disconfirmation" (courts). (27:37-30:43) — opinion, partly evidence
- [AI][Q] Examples of useful adversarial loops: a designer-perspective agent that keeps comparing the vibe-coded output to the Figma; QA agents that use the product at runtime. (32:46) — practice, aspirational
- [BIZ] Roles are shaped as much by conflicting interests as by skills: designers push for better, managers for on-time launch; product evolution comes from a balance of production, evaluation and synthesis. (30:43-31:44) — opinion

## Techniques and steps
1. Do not rely on one huge prompt or PRD; split the work and keep context small and relevant (17:24-19:26).
2. Compress long chat history before passing it on (19:26).
3. Treat every agent output as unreviewed: test, lint, and have a second perspective check it (10:18, 32:46).
4. Set up separate reviewer roles (design, QA, engineering) with different incentives and let them argue before you accept (26:35-32:46).

## Disagreements and contrarian points
Against spec-driven development ("waterfall"). Against single-agent chain-of-thought as the model of reasoning. Skeptical of the "agents will replace us" story and of benchmark-based progress claims, while still optimistic about orchestration. He accepts the data that AI beats most humans at idea generation, and draws from it that human value shifts to taste and evaluation (a view shared with many AI-slop talks).

## For 1000 Islands
Direct lessons for AI-assisted HTML building: (1) keep sessions short and the context file lean (DESIGN.md and AGENTS.md small; start a fresh session when regressions appear; context rot is a real cause of "page got worse on edit 5"); (2) never accept generated sections without a check: our gates (detector, fit-check, final-check) are exactly the "evaluator loop"; (3) AI can produce many layout variants cheaply, so the owner's and our scarce resource is picking and judging, i.e. taste; (4) do not write a big up-front spec for the whole site; iterate with the clickable prototype. A "designer agent" comparing output to the approved wireframe or references is a plausible check to add.
