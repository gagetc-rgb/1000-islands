## Core thesis
Memorable work comes from deliberately making people feel something (surprise, humour, calm, "cool"), not from flawless efficiency alone. Find one motif (e.g. "start fast, end slow") early and let every decision follow it; collect small moments from other products so you can reuse the instinct.

## Claims and advice
- [EMO] His design goal is to recreate the feeling of his first Mac boot video and the game Metal Gear Solid: surprise, humanity, "you can feel the people behind it". (1:00-4:02) — opinion
- [EMO] Moments that break the fourth wall or make the user "get it" after frustration are remembered forever (Metal Gear Solid: the phone frequency printed on the back of the game box; boss defeated by swapping controller ports). (4:02-6:05) — evidence (anecdote)
- [EMO] "Surprise and delight" is overused but still the thing: inject some of yourself, create small "oh wow / how did they do that" moments. (7:06) — opinion
- [EMO][MB] Big companies cannot afford risky, targeted experiences because they design for everyone; small indie teams and side projects can. If you generalise for every user you cannot do special moments. (11:13-16:19) — opinion
- [EMO][Q] Test data from the Retro intro video (haptic slideshow before sign-up): they lost roughly 20-30% of sign-ups at the first step, but the end-of-onboarding difference was much softer; they then made it skippable (first tap-and-hold, then easy skip) and respected the phone's mute switch. (13:15-15:18) — evidence (self-reported, no deep testing)
- [Q][EMO] Do not let edge cases (the 1% persona or state) lower the ceiling of the experience for the audience you actually optimise for; downplay, not ignore. Add friction if it builds affinity for the core persona. (17:20) — opinion
- [Q] Unlearn some old UX dogmas (no scrolling, labels everywhere, pagination): "people know how to scroll". Apple removed the iOS home indicator without backlash as an example of trusting users. (18:21-19:23) — opinion
- [P][EMO] Process of finding the motif: start with what you know how to do (slideshow, haptics), study ad-campaign slideshows (no fades, hard cuts, slow zoom on the whole thing rather than per photo), build, let teammates play it, then notice what they felt (pacing). The idea "start fast, slow down" came from a coworker. Once the motif exists, soundtrack and visuals follow it (street noise fades out, music remains). (20:24-24:27) — practice
- [EMO] "No genius": start from the doable, talk to people, accept accidents. Days may go into finding the motif, then everything unravels from it. (22:25-24:27) — practice
- [CL][P] To communicate an idea, make a video, not mockups: under 10 minutes, ideally about 5; add a light soundtrack and switch music when the topic switches; add humour; "make an ad, sell this idea". Static mockup loses the most information. (31:33-34:35) — practice
- [CL][EMO] For emotional features, show real people using it; film a hand holding a real phone at a slight angle (more real than a simulator screen recording), film B-roll of the use context (coming home, sitting at a desk), show faces reacting. (39:39-40:41) — practice
- [EMO] Humour is part of UX: his site hero has him leaning in and waving (green screen), then getting awkward if you linger and leaving; humorous touches are what make his side projects good. (47:50-51:56) — opinion
- [EMO] If you make people feel something it is more memorable; people remember feelings, not "fast workflow of 10 years ago". Speed and clarity still matter, but emotion is what stays. (48:53-49:54) — opinion
- [EMO] Don't force funny or meaningful: put yourself in a state to notice "wouldn't it be funny / cool?", then prototype it cheaply. Empty states are good places for small moments. (51:56-53:57) — practice
- [AI][EMO] With AI building, tiny delights take seconds (hover on "sign in" changes text and a waving emoji says "Oh, hi Rafa" took 30 seconds), so "why not". (54:59) — practice
- [REF] For beginners: recreate designs you like in Figma/Sketch to learn padding and layout by tracing; and keep a folder or note of small interactions seen elsewhere (hover wave, vector signature at the end of a blog post) to reuse later. (55:00-58:03) — practice
- [P] Prototyping choice: Figma vs a separate code project vs a branch of the main app depends on what's fastest; he is most efficient in an isolated code project. At Retro everyone (design, marketing) now builds prototypes and opens PRs with AI tools, with some mess when prototypes go to production. (29:32-31:33) — practice

## Techniques and steps
1. Pick a feeling you want the person to have, then look for a single motif or metaphor that carries it (e.g. pace slows down).
2. Prototype with what you know, let others try it, listen to what they felt, refine toward the motif.
3. Align all channels with the motif (visual pacing, sound, haptics).
4. Make the experience skippable and test the drop-off.
5. Share ideas as a short, scrappy, entertaining video: voice-over, soundtrack, humour, B-roll with a real phone.
6. Keep a swipe file of tiny moments from other products and use them next time.

## Disagreements and contrarian points
Against designing for everyone and removing all risk; against over-protecting the 1% edge case; against "stay serious" professional tone (humour as UX); against static mockups for communication. Acknowledges the cost: his intro video lost sign-ups.

## For 1000 Islands
Most of this is app-specific, but three things transfer: (1) choose one emotional motif for the site (e.g. "slow down, go quiet") before choosing visuals, and let photography pacing, copy and motion follow it; (2) one or two quiet surprise moments are affordable now with AI-built HTML (a small hover or scroll detail), but keep them skippable and not in front of the booking path; (3) when presenting directions to the owner, a short scrappy walkthrough video may land better than static screens. Do not bury the main action behind a long intro (his sign-up loss). Keep a swipe file of micro-interactions from travel sites.
