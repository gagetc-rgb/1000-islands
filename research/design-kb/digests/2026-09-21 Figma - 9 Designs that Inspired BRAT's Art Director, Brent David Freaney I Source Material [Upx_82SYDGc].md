## Core thesis
Two kinds of references feed his taste: foundational design books (colour, information display, type) and found everyday objects made without designers (boarding passes, church pamphlets, manifestos). He prefers the "undesigned" and the human over smooth, friendly, opinionless design.

## Claims and advice
- [REF] He collects two kinds of things: core typographic and visual books, and objects picked up along the way that "tone" his aesthetic. (0:04) — practice
- [VIS-C] Joseph Albers "Interaction of Color" for how colour changes depending on neighbours; plus the "brutal" layouts of colour, shape and scale. (0:04-1:04) — opinion
- [VIS-C] A colour-combination dictionary is a "Bible" that pushes him to use hues he normally avoids. (1:04) — practice
- [VIS-C][P] He avoids choosing colours starting on the computer: he gathers physical objects from home, office and street, rearranges them in the studio, then translates into digital. (1:04-2:06) — practice
- [VIS-C] i-D has used colour since the 1980s to lighten mood and give an upbeat energy. (1:04) — evidence
- [REF][AIS] He is interested in design that graphic designers did not design: pamphlets by a church volunteer with a template program, utility documents like a Thai airport boarding pass printed on a wall of printers (carbon copy). (2:06-3:08) — opinion
- [AIS] Names a trend "the sweet-greenification of design": everything must look like an app, friendly, pleasing and without opinion. (4:10) — opinion
- [VIS-C] He built his studio's colour palette from a 1990s McDonald's brand guideline, after Takashi Homma's zine of zoomed-in McDonald's photographs; interest is the abstraction of colours working together. (3:08-4:10) — practice
- [REF] A 1980s Park Hyatt Tokyo room-service menu as a window on how design looked in another country in that era. (4:10-5:10) — practice
- [VIS-T] He rarely likes italics and tries annually to find one that works. Core type styles: Franklin Gothic (used since his first website in high school), Helvetica, sometimes Arial; Bruce Weber's large, tightly tracked long-form text as ideal. (5:10, 8:13) — opinion/practice
- [VIS-L] Edward Tufte's information-display books: understanding how to lay out and show information clearly. (5:10-6:11) — opinion
- [AIS][EMO] "Defaultness" and human quality (a typewriter-set manifesto with Sharpie drawings) matter; with AI and tools that let anyone design, the human part is what to hold on to and sharpen. (7:13) — opinion
- [VIS-I][EMO] The physical object of a book (weight, texture, how it sits) is part of design that content-focused readers do not notice. (9:15) — opinion

## Techniques and steps
1. Collect physical colour finds (objects, packaging) and arrange them on a table before touching software.
2. Use a colour-combination reference when stuck on hues.
3. Study non-designer documents for unselfconscious type and layout.

## Tools and resources
- Books: Albers "Interaction of Color", a colour-combinations dictionary, Tufte "Visual Display of Quantitative Information", Bruce Weber "Is Love Enough", Takashi Homma McDonald's zine; typefaces Franklin Gothic, Helvetica; 1990s McDonald's brand guideline

## Disagreements and contrarian points
Against smooth, friendly, "app-like" design; against starting colour on the computer; for the "brutal" and unauthored.

## For 1000 Islands
Mild help for Stage 2: take palette ideas from physical objects of the region (boat paint, ferry signs, stone, water at different hours) rather than from digital palette tools; look at non-designer printed travel material (old ferry timetables, postcards) for honest type; beware the generic friendly-app look we want to avoid. Low priority.
