## Core thesis
Landing pages move through four levels of craft, from template-like (level 1) to cohesive and detailed (level 4). The difference between levels is not talent but a set of decisions: layout variety, hierarchy, tailored visuals, copy that says how it helps, and motion detail. Level 4 needs no custom illustration or 3D, only well-crafted UI and attention to detail.

## Claims and advice
- [Q][VIS-L] Level 1 (2/10, "template", worth about $500): alternating text-left/image-right all the way down, stock images unrelated to the product, sharp image corners against rounded buttons, no variety. Functionally fine, "no design decisions whatsoever". (0:00-2:02) - type: opinion
- [VIS-T][Q] Level 1 typography: one font is fine, but hierarchy is weak and the words are vague and longer than needed; no one reads a block of text; do not repeat the company name when the logo is right there. (1:00) - type: opinion
- [Q] Level 1 navigation: every menu item looks the same, no emphasis on sign-up or key links, right-heavy; one accent blue spread on everything that can take it; color leans on images to do the work and misses. (1:00-2:02) - type: opinion
- [VIS-M] Level 1 animation: only a simple hover; nothing that brings the site to life. (2:02) - type: opinion
- [Q][VIS-L] Level 2 (6/10, about $2,000): stacked hero instead of side by side, real product dashboard instead of a stock image, color comes from the product visual, white buttons on dark, tighter typography, shorter punchier headline, outlined primary menu button, centered links, small hover dropdowns, simple load animations, a logical top-to-bottom flow though section transitions are abrupt. (2:02-4:05) - type: opinion
- [Q] Same label on matching CTAs (top-left and hero button) tells users both go to the same place: the "correct mental model". (3:04) - type: practice
- [Q][VIS-L] Level 3 (8/10, about $6-10k): zoom in on the important parts of the product visual to curate what the viewer sees; vertical lines framing the content help it scale on very large screens; a Bento grid replaces a row of four cards; a multi-select tab interaction replaces a three-card row; shorter, more compact sections. (4:05-5:05) - type: opinion
- [VIS-T][Q] Level 3 hierarchy through color and size; copy short and describes the product as a whole; slightly overdone (headline dominates the sub-line). Designers become "product designers" by adding brand-strengthening elements: a badge, a logo set, a button to a social-proof page. (5:05-6:06) - type: opinion
- [Q][VIS-M] Level 3 adds a mega menu (signals depth), smoother animations, subtle hover on dashboard and Bento, a fluid slider; color use may fall as visuals are zoomed and charts removed; page flow is better but not seamless. (6:06-7:06) - type: opinion
- [Q] Level 4 (10/10, "well over $10,000"): visuals crafted to show exactly what the product does; more flexible layouts (not just straight lines); looser spacing and widths forming "leading lines" into content; "the difference between good and great is almost never one big thing". (7:06) - type: opinion
- [EMO][VIS-T] Copy shift from "what it does" to "how it helps": e.g. "collecting and analyzing data quickly" becomes a promise of turning data into decisions. (7:06-8:08) - type: practice

## Techniques and steps
Self-audit checklist implied by the levels:
1. Layout: vary section layouts; avoid pure alternating left-right; try stacked heroes and Bento grids.
2. Visuals: replace stock images with the real product; zoom and crop to what matters.
3. Typography and copy: one clear hierarchy; short headline; say how it helps, not just what it does.
4. Navigation: emphasise the main action; match CTA labels; consider a mega menu if depth exists.
5. Color: take it from the product visual, apply with restraint, make one CTA color primary.
6. Motion: load animation, smooth hovers, then refined transitions (blur, persistent menus, hover-revealed CTAs).
7. Flow: make section transitions logical and continuous, from logo strip to features.

## Disagreements and contrarian points
Mostly consistent with mainstream critique culture. One slightly contrarian note: advanced pages can lose color and flow when they get more restrained; and a pretty page still needs polished motion to feel premium.

## For 1000 Islands
Useful as a craft audit even though it is about SaaS: vary the layout between sections, replace generic stock with specific real photography of the place (the travel analogue of "show the real product"), make the headline say how the trip feels or helps ("how it helps"), keep matching CTA labels, one primary action color, and add one or two refined motion details rather than many. Level 4's "no illustrations or 3D, just attention to detail" fits our small scope. Do not copy SaaS patterns such as dashboards, mega menus or Bento grids unless they serve a small tour site.
