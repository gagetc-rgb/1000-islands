## Core thesis
Every new technology in history triggers a moral panic that is partly about real pain and partly a fight over who defines value; today's words "slop" and "taste" are the 2026 version of "corrupting" and "vulgar". The critics are often right about the wound and wrong about the prescription. A disruption earns a qualified "yes" only if it adds new capability, spreads the upside, and builds believable new ladders for displaced people; AI clears the first bar, is unproven on the second, and fails the third for juniors.

## Claims and advice
- [AIS] Pattern across coffee houses, cheap print, bicycles, pinball, comic books, tabletop and video games: a new thing spreads first among "the wrong social groups" (children, women, outsiders), then the establishment reaches for moral language. Today's vocabulary is "slop" and "taste". (1:02) — opinion
- [AIS] The panic is "always also about a fight over who gets to define value": the taste/slop discourse is partly status language, not only a quality judgment. (1:02-2:03) — opinion
- [AIS] Critics were often right about the real danger (rail crashes and "railway spine" trauma; early electrification deaths), but prescriptions are often self-serving (Edison funding public demonstrations of lethal AC while losing to Westinghouse and Tesla). (2:03-4:06) — evidence (historical anecdotes, told briefly)
- [BIZ] Photography took over a commercial function (affordable portraits); painters who held the old function absorbed the hit while the medium grew and redefined itself (Impressionism). "Photography killed painting" is oversimplified. (4:06-5:06) — opinion/evidence
- [BIZ] The original Luddites (1811) were skilled textile workers resisting wage cuts and degraded craft; Cambridge is cited as framing them as protesting how owners used the technology, not technology itself. (5:06) — evidence
- [BIZ] Desktop publishing (PageMaker plus Apple LaserWriter, 1985) and Wapping (Murdoch, 1986, about 6,000 dismissed) and CAD (drafters' discretion disappeared) show one pattern: the tool is chosen by owners, the cost is absorbed by practitioners. (5:06-7:09) — evidence
- [AI][BIZ] Three-bar test for whether a disruption is worth it: (1) new capability, (2) diffusion of upside beyond first owners, (3) believable new ladders for the displaced. AI clears bar 1; bar 2 is open; bar 3 (junior ladder) is the one he worries about most. (8:09-12:15) — opinion (his own framework)
- [BIZ] Stanford analysis (cited via Time): early-career workers in highly AI-exposed occupations down nearly 16% since 2022. (10:13) — evidence (secondary citation, not verified here)
- [BIZ] Ramp Economics Lab analysis (as cited): more than half of firms paying freelancers in 2022 no longer did in 2025, spend shifted to AI; at the high end AI was "25 times cheaper" than freelance spend. (10:13-11:13) — evidence (secondary citation)
- [BIZ] Figma data (as cited): demand for designers remains, but hiring skews senior and entry points narrow. Open question: where does future senior judgment get built? (11:13) — evidence
- [AI] He uses AI daily, believes it "expands human freedom", and urges building new ladders rather than either panic or "we've been here before, relax". (13:15-14:16) — opinion

## Disagreements and contrarian points
Against the dismissive "calm down, we've been here before" camp: the pain is real and may be faster and broader this time. Equally against pure doom: capability gains are real. Takes a "qualified yes" stance.
